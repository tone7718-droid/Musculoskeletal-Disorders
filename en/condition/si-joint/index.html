Sacroiliac (SI) Joint Dysfunction

The joint where the sacrum meets the pelvis is overloaded, so one side below the belt line aches.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The sacroiliac joint, where the end of the spine (sacrum) meets the pelvic bone (ilium), is the body's 'cornerstone' joint. As the crossing point where upper-body weight transfers to the legs, it bears large forces but moves only a few millimeters — strong ligaments bind it tightly.

Repeated asymmetric load (standing on one leg, crossing the legs, childbirth, a fall) irritates the joint and ligaments into pain. It's easily confused with a lumbar disc, but the pain location differs — you can point a finger at the sacrum below the belt line, slightly to the side of the spine.

It's especially common in women around pregnancy/childbirth, when hormones loosen the ligaments while the pelvis is loaded.

Why does it happen?

  • Asymmetric habits — standing on one leg, crossing legs, always carrying a baby on one side
  • Pregnancy/childbirth (ligament laxity plus increased pelvic load)
  • Falling on the buttocks, a fall, a car accident
  • Leg-length difference, one-sided rotational sports (golf, bowling)
  • A stiff hip or back making the SI joint overwork instead

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Pain on one side of the sacrum below the belt, beside the spine (you can point to it)
  • A twinge the moment you stand from a chair or step out of a car
  • Instability on the affected side when standing on one leg or climbing stairs
  • Worse with long standing or long sitting
  • Painful to roll over, uncomfortable lying on the affected side
  • Pain sometimes spreading to the buttock, groin, or back of the thigh (rarely below the knee)

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 One-finger test

Point to where it hurts with a single finger.

For reference: If you point exactly to the bony dimple above the buttock and below the belt (around the PSIS), the SI joint is likely. Disc pain is usually broad and vague.

🔍 FABER test

Lying down, put the affected ankle on the opposite knee in a figure-4 and gently press the bent knee toward the floor.

For reference: Reproduced pain behind the sacrum suggests SI joint irritation (front-groin pain points to a hip problem instead).

🔍 Single-leg stand comparison

Stand on each leg for 10 seconds.

For reference: If standing on the affected side clearly brings sacral pain or instability, it suggests an SI joint and surrounding muscle problem.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Manual therapy — pelvic alignment correction & mobilization

The therapist assesses pelvic and SI movement, gently mobilizes the stiff direction, and releases tense surrounding muscles (piriformis, quadratus lumborum, etc.).

🤲 Pelvic stabilization belt

A belt gently compressing the pelvis supports the loosened joint and reduces pain. Especially useful for postpartum pain, as a 'temporary prop' until strength returns.

🤲 Injection (by a doctor)

If pain is severe and lasting, an intra-articular injection calms inflammation and can also confirm the diagnosis (if pain disappears, the SI joint is the source).

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Glute bridge

Lie with knees bent, squeeze the glutes, and lift the pelvis, holding 5 seconds. The glutes are a 'natural belt' for the SI joint.

⏱ 10 reps × 3 sets, once a day

🏃 Clamshell (outer-hip strengthening)

Lie on your side with knees bent and feet together, opening just the top knee like a clam. Keep the pelvis from rolling back.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets (each side), once a day

🏃 Abdominal bracing + bird-dog

Keep a light abdominal brace (about 30% of the firmness when you cough) while reaching opposite arm and leg on all fours. Trains the trunk and pelvis to move as one.

⏱ 10 sec × 5 reps (each side), once a day

🏃 Correcting asymmetric habits

No standing on one leg or crossing the legs, no wallet in a back pocket while sitting, carry a baby on alternating sides. Cutting the irritating cause matters as much as exercise.

⏱ Lifestyle — always

🏃 Piriformis / hip stretch

Lie down, make a figure-4, and hug the lower thigh to stretch deep in the buttock 30 seconds. Only to a comfortable stretch, not pain.

⏱ 30 sec × 3 reps (each side), twice a day

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • Morning stiffness lasting 30–60+ minutes that eases with movement, with sacral pain for months (rule out inflammatory disease like ankylosing spondylitis — especially ages 20–40)
  • Fever, weight loss, or pain waking you at night
  • Leg tingling/weakness going below the knee (rule out a back nerve problem)
  • Can't bear weight after a fall/accident (rule out a fracture)

⚠️ This material is for health information only and does not replace a doctor's diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms last more than 2 weeks or any red flag applies, see a medical professional.
