Shoulder Instability / Dislocation

The structures holding the shoulder loosen or are damaged, so the shoulder dislocates or feels unstable as if it will pop out.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body, and so the most easily dislocated. Like a golf ball on a tee, a large ball sits on a shallow socket, held by the labrum (rim cartilage), ligaments, and muscles. If these stabilizers are badly injured once (traumatic dislocation) or are naturally loose (atraumatic), the shoulder moves beyond its normal range and pops out or feels unstable.

A once-dislocated shoulder, especially in the young, recurs very often — because a torn labrum (Bankart lesion) from the first dislocation makes the next ones easier. Conversely, generally loose shoulders without trauma are managed mainly by building stability with exercise.

Anterior (forward) dislocation is most common, with the most apprehension in the abducted-and-externally-rotated position (throwing, raising the arm).

Why does it happen?

  • Trauma — falling on an outstretched arm or the arm being forced back (sports, falls)
  • Recurrent dislocation from labrum/ligament damage after the first one
  • Inborn general joint laxity (a flexible body)
  • Repeated forceful overhead sport (swimming, volleyball, throwing)
  • Weak surrounding muscles (rotator cuff, scapula)

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • The shoulder has dislocated before, or feels like it will pop out
  • Especially unstable in the abducted-externally-rotated position
  • A 'clunk' or shifting feeling in certain movements
  • Occasional weakness or a tingling arm
  • Apprehension or pain with the arm overhead when sleeping
  • An aching, heavy shoulder after exercise

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Apprehension position (carefully)

Seated, raise the arm 90° out to the side with the elbow bent 90°, then slowly bring the hand back (externally rotate). Never force it to the end.

For reference: A feeling that it 'will pop out' in this position suggests anterior instability. The key sign is apprehension, not pain.

🔍 General laxity check

Check whether the thumb pulls back toward the wrist and whether the elbows and little fingers hyperextend.

For reference: If several joints over-extend, you have inborn laxity, suggesting an atraumatic instability tendency.

🔍 History check

Recall whether the shoulder has actually dislocated, how many times, and your age at the first one.

For reference: A young first traumatic dislocation with repeats raises the chance of structural damage — get an accurate diagnosis.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Acute dislocation reduction (medical staff only)

If the shoulder is out, don't try to relocate it yourself — go to a hospital immediately for reduction. There's a risk of nerve/vessel injury, so professional handling is essential.

🤲 Manual therapy — alignment & muscle balance

The therapist aligns the blade and shoulder, releases the over-tense parts, and cues the weak stabilizers to work so the shoulder stays centered.

🤲 Surgical consult (if needed)

For clear recurrent dislocations or a labral tear, arthroscopic repair of the torn labrum greatly reduces recurrence. The decision depends on age, activity level, and damage.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Rotator-cuff strengthening (band external/internal rotation)

Elbow at your side, slowly rotate the band out and in. Builds the stabilizers that press the ball into the socket. Avoid the unstable (abducted-externally-rotated) position — stay in a safe range.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets, once a day

🏃 Scapular stabilization (rows, Y-T-W)

Band rows and prone Y-T-W stabilize the shoulder's 'launch pad,' the blade. A firm base means fewer dislocations.

⏱ 12–15 reps × 3 sets each, once a day

🏃 Proprioception training (weight-bearing)

Place a hand on a wall or table, load lightly, and draw small circles, or shift your center of mass in a stable push-up position. Re-educates the shoulder's position sense.

⏱ 1–2 min × 2–3 sets, once a day

🏃 Progressive strengthening in a safe range

Start at angles without pain or apprehension and gradually widen range and weight. Athletes return stepwise with sport-specific movements.

⏱ 3 times a week, progressive

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • The shoulder is dislocated and won't go back (go to the ER immediately — don't relocate it yourself)
  • Tingling, weakness, or altered sensation in the arm/hand after dislocation (nerve/vessel injury)
  • Recurrent dislocations disrupting daily life and sleep (surgery consult)
  • Severe swelling, deformity, and pain after trauma (rule out a fracture)

⚠️ This material is for health information only and does not replace a doctor's diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms last more than 2 weeks or any red flag applies, see a medical professional.
