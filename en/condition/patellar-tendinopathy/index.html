Patellar Tendinopathy (Jumper's Knee)

The tendon just below the kneecap wears from repeated jumping and landing, so one point at the front of the knee hurts sharply.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The patellar tendon connects the kneecap to the shin bone and works as a spring, transmitting several times body weight each time you jump and land. With repeated jumping (volleyball, basketball, jump rope), the tendon's healing can't keep up with the damage, and part of it turns degenerated like an old rope.

The biggest difference from patellofemoral pain (runner's knee) is location — jumper's knee lets you point a finger at 'one spot just below the kneecap.' Another hallmark is the 'warm-up phenomenon': pain eases once you start exercising and returns after you cool down.

A shared rule of tendon problems: complete rest weakens the tendon further, while appropriately weighted exercise rebuilds it stronger.

Why does it happen?

  • Repeated jumping, landing, cutting (volleyball, basketball, badminton, jump rope)
  • A sudden increase in training (season start, competition prep)
  • Poor thigh/calf flexibility and landing technique
  • Repeated training on hard surfaces
  • In adolescents, similar-area conditions (e.g. Osgood-Schlatter) are also common

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • A single point just below the kneecap hurts sharply
  • Pain jumping, landing, going down stairs, squatting
  • Pain at the start of exercise that eases as you warm up and returns after
  • Clear tenderness pressing the sore spot
  • Stiffness below the knee when standing up after long sitting
  • If severe, pain on everyday stairs

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Single-point tenderness

With the leg comfortably straight, press where the bottom of the kneecap meets the tendon.

For reference: Clear reproduced pain at that one spot makes patellar tendinopathy likely.

🔍 Decline-squat test

Put the heels on a book or a 25° wedge and slowly single-leg squat down and up.

For reference: Reproduced pain at the single spot below the kneecap is positive; record the pain score (0–10) to track recovery.

🔍 Warm-up phenomenon check

Record pain before, during, and after exercise.

For reference: A pattern of pain at the start → decreasing during → increasing after / next morning is classic for a tendon problem.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Shockwave (ESWT)

Shockwaves to the worn area of the chronic patellar tendon stimulate healing. Effective combined with exercise.

🤲 Manual therapy — thigh & calf release

The therapist releases the stiff front-thigh and calf muscles to reduce the pull on the tendon. The aim is to lighten the surrounding load, not to rub the tendon itself.

🤲 Patellar tendon strap

A strap below the kneecap helps spread the tendon load, reducing pain during exercise — an aid so you can do rehab pain-free.

🤲 Steroid injections — cautious

Steroid injection into the patellar tendon can raise rupture risk and is generally not recommended. If offered an injection, confirm the type and rationale.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Stage 1 — isometric holds (pain calming)

In a Spanish-squat position against a wall with the knee bent ~60°, or on a leg-extension machine, hold 30–45 seconds without moving. A proven way to reduce tendon pain.

⏱ 45 sec × 5 reps, 1–2 times a day

🏃 Stage 2 — heavy slow resistance (HSR)

Do squats and leg presses at a slow '3 seconds down, 3 seconds up,' progressively heavier. If pain stays under 3, you can add weight. The key stage that re-organizes the tendon tissue.

⏱ 8–12 reps (build to heavier 6) × 3–4 sets, 3 times a week

🏃 Stage 3 — decline squat (eccentric emphasis)

Heels on a wedge, single-leg lower over 3 seconds, then come up on both legs.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets, 3 times a week (alternate with Stage 2)

🏃 Stage 4 — return-to-jump training

Two-foot stationary jump → single-leg jump → repeated jumps → cutting, advancing each stage while checking pain and next-morning reaction.

⏱ 1–2 weeks per stage

🏃 Flexibility care

Make front-thigh, hamstring, and calf stretches a post-exercise routine.

⏱ 30 sec each × 2 reps, after every session

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • A 'pop' during a jump or sprint after which you can't straighten the knee (tendon rupture — emergency)
  • The whole knee swollen and warm
  • An adolescent with a painful bump below the knee (Osgood-Schlatter — needs growth-stage care)
  • No improvement after 6+ months of staged rehab
