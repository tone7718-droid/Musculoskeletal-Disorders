🦿 Knee

Iliotibial Band Syndrome (outer-knee pain)

The long band of fascia on the outer thigh irritates the outer knee, so a stabbing pain hits the outer knee partway through a run.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The iliotibial (IT) band is a long, tough strip of fascia running from the pelvis to the outer knee. As you bend and straighten the knee, the lower part of the band passes over a bony bump on the outer knee. With running's thousands of bend-straighten cycles, the tissue under the band gets compressed and irritated into inflammation.

It's the #1 cause of outer-knee pain in runners. The hallmark is 'predictable timing' — after a set distance into a run (say 3 km) the outer knee starts hurting like clockwork; it eases when you stop but returns at the same point when you run again. Downhill is especially bad.

A common misconception: the IT band isn't a tissue you 'stretch loose.' It's tough as a steel belt and barely lengthens with stretching; the real problem is weakness of the hip muscles that tension it and running habits.

Why does it happen?

  • A sudden increase in running distance or frequency
  • Downhill running, always running the same direction on a track
  • Weak outer-hip muscles (gluteus medius) dropping the pelvis and caving the knee in when running
  • A narrow step width (feet landing on a single line)
  • Bike saddle/cleat setup issues (cyclists)

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Sharp, stabbing pain on the outer knee (around the bony bump)
  • Pain that appears like clockwork after a set distance into a run
  • Worse going downhill or down stairs
  • Eases when you stop but returns at the same point when running again
  • Worst when the knee is bent about 30°
  • Tenderness pressing the outer knee

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Tenderness location check

Press around the bony bump just above the joint line on the outer knee.

For reference: Clear tenderness there that matches where it hurts when running makes it likely. (Pain on the joint line itself needs a meniscus check.)

🔍 Noble compression test

Lying down, have someone press the bony bump on the outer knee with the thumb while you slowly straighten the knee from 90°.

For reference: Reproduced familiar pain around 30° is positive.

🔍 Single-leg squat pelvis watch

In a mirror, do a mini-squat on the affected leg and watch the height of the opposite pelvis.

For reference: If the opposite pelvis drops sharply, that's gluteus medius weakness — the most common root of ITBS.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Manual therapy — TFL & glute release

The therapist releases the muscles that tension the IT band (the tensor fasciae latae at the front-outer thigh and the glutes) to lower the band's tension. Releasing the band's 'owner muscles' beats rubbing the band itself.

🤲 Inflammation management

In the acute phase, ice and, if needed, doctor-prescribed anti-inflammatories calm the inflammation on the outer knee. An injection is sometimes used for severe pain.

🤲 Running form / equipment check

Get a running-gait analysis to check step width, cadence, and shoe wear. For cyclists, a bike fit is part of the treatment.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Gluteus medius strengthening (clamshell → banded walks → single-leg)

Start with clamshells and side leg raises, then banded side-walks and single-leg squats. The #1 priority of ITBS rehab.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets each, once a day

🏃 Hip hinge & single-leg deadlift

Stand on one leg and hinge the torso forward while extending the other leg back, building the hip strength to control landing while running.

⏱ 10 reps × 3 sets (each side), 3 times a week

🏃 Foam-roll the front-outer thigh

Instead of painfully grinding the IT band itself, gently roll the muscle area below the outer pelvis (TFL) and the front of the thigh.

⏱ 1–2 min, once a day

🏃 Running-habit correction

Widen the step slightly so feet land hip-width apart rather than on one line, and raise cadence 5–10%. Avoid downhills and one-way track loops for now.

⏱ Apply when returning to running

🏃 Graded return to running

Start at 70% of your pain-free distance, every other day, increasing under 10% a week. If pain appears, drop back to the distance before it.

⏱ 3 times a week, the 10% rule

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • Pain and swelling on the joint line (bone-to-bone), or locking (rule out a meniscus tear)
  • Onset after trauma with knee instability (rule out a ligament injury)
  • A swollen, warm knee
  • No improvement after 8+ weeks of exercise therapy and running adjustment
