Posterior Tibial Tendon Dysfunction (adult acquired flatfoot)

The tendon that supports the inner arch weakens, so the arch gradually collapses and the inner ankle/foot hurts.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The posterior tibial tendon runs down the inner calf, wraps behind the inner ankle bone, and attaches to the sole's arch — the 'main beam of the arch,' lifting and supporting it and stabilizing the foot when walking. When this tendon weakens from overload/degeneration, it can't hold the arch, and a previously normal foot gradually collapses into a flatfoot — posterior tibial tendon dysfunction.

Common in women after middle age, the obese, and those with diabetes. Early on, behind and below the inner ankle bone swells and hurts; as it progresses, the arch caves and the heel turns outward, so from behind you see 'too many toes' on the outer side.

Caught early, insoles/braces and exercise can halt progression; left late, the deformity stiffens and may need surgery — so respond early.

Why does it happen?

  • Repeated overload and age-related degeneration of the posterior tibial tendon
  • Obesity (increased load on the arch)
  • Middle-aged women, with conditions like diabetes and hypertension
  • Long standing/walking, a sudden increase in activity
  • A foot that already tended toward flatness

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Swelling and pain behind and below the inner ankle bone
  • Inner-foot pain and quick fatigue after long standing or walking
  • The arch lower than before, becoming a flatfoot
  • Difficulty or inability doing a single-leg heel raise
  • The heel turning outward, and 'too many toes' visible from behind
  • As it progresses, the outer foot (below the ankle bone) also hurts

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Single-leg heel-raise test (key)

Hold a wall and try to rise onto the toes on one leg. Compare both sides.

For reference: If you can't heel-raise on the affected side, or the heel doesn't turn inward as you rise, it strongly suggests posterior tibial tendon dysfunction.

🔍 Arch/heel alignment watch

Stand barefoot and compare arch height and heel direction in a mirror or photo.

For reference: If one arch is lower with the heel turned out, or more toes show from behind, it suggests progressing deformity.

🔍 Tenderness/swelling check

Press along the tendon behind the inner ankle bone.

For reference: Swelling and tenderness along that line make tendon inflammation/dysfunction likely.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Custom insole / arch brace (first-line)

An insole/brace that supports the arch and holds the heel straight reduces the tendon's load, easing pain and slowing progression. The core early treatment; advanced deformity may need a custom brace.

🤲 Manual therapy — calf/ankle release & mobilization

The therapist releases the stiff calf (Achilles) and restores ankle motion to reduce the load on the arch and aid foot alignment.

🤲 Acute protection & medication

If pain/swelling is severe, reduce activity and, if needed, protect briefly with a walking boot, calming inflammation with doctor-prescribed anti-inflammatories. Advanced deformity is discussed for surgical correction.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Heel raises (posterior tibial strengthening, key)

Start with two-leg heel raises, driving weight toward the inner (big-toe) side so the heel turns inward as you rise. Progress to single-leg. Slowly (3 sec up, 3 sec down).

⏱ 10–15 reps × 3 sets, every other day

🏃 Foot inward pull (band inversion)

Loop a band on the inner foot and pull the foot in and down, then return slowly. Strengthens the posterior tibial directly.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets, once a day

🏃 Calf (Achilles) stretch

Wall-push with the knee straight and bent to stretch the calf. A stiff calf increases arch load.

⏱ 30 sec each × 3 reps, twice a day

🏃 Foot intrinsic exercise (short-foot, toe work)

Short-foot exercise (drawing the arch up short) and toe-scrunching a towel build the foot's core to support the arch.

⏱ 10 reps × 2–3 sets each, once a day

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • No single-leg heel raise at all and a rapidly collapsing arch (progressing — see a doctor quickly)
  • Deformity stiffening so the ankle tilts to one side
  • The inner foot suddenly very swollen, red, and hot (rule out infection/inflammation)
  • You have diabetes and a wound or ulcer forms (diabetic foot — see a doctor immediately)
  • Pain/deformity progressing despite months of braces and exercise (surgery consult)
