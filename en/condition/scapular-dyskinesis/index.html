Scapular Dyskinesis

When you raise your arm, the shoulder blade doesn't move in its proper rhythm — a hidden cause behind much shoulder pain.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The shoulder blade (scapula) is the 'launch pad' for the arm. When you raise your arm overhead, it isn't just the arm bone moving — the shoulder blade rotates and travels along with it (the scapulohumeral rhythm). Just as a rocket can't fly straight from a wobbly pad, a poorly moving shoulder blade piles abnormal stress on the shoulder joint.

Scapular dyskinesis isn't so much a 'diagnosis' as a broken movement pattern from weakness and imbalance of the muscles that steer the blade (serratus anterior, lower trapezius, etc.). It's very often the starting point of impingement and rotator-cuff problems, so it's always addressed in shoulder rehab.

Why does it happen?

  • Rounded back / forward head tilting and winging the shoulder blade forward
  • Weakness of the serratus anterior and lower trapezius (the blade stabilizers)
  • A habit of overusing only the upper trapezius (the shrug pattern)
  • A faulty movement habit learned to avoid pain after a shoulder injury
  • A short chest muscle (pectoralis minor) pulling the blade forward

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Aching at the back of the shoulder or around the blade when raising or lowering the arm
  • In the mirror, one shoulder blade looks more prominent or drooped
  • The shoulder shrugs up and the neck works first when you raise the arm
  • The area around the blade tires unusually after exercise (pull-ups, bench press, swimming)
  • Frequent grinding or clicking in the shoulder
  • The inner edge of the blade feels cold and achy after sitting a long time

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Mirror shoulder-blade watch

Take off your shirt and watch your back in a mirror or photo while slowly raising both arms forward and lowering them, 3–5 times.

For reference: If the inner edge or lower tip of one blade wings out, the two sides move clearly differently, or one blade 'drops' on the way down, suspect dyskinesis.

🔍 Wall push-up test

Make a push-up position against a wall, then bend toward and push away from the wall 10 times. Have someone watch your shoulder blades.

For reference: If one blade lifts off the back and wings out as you push away, that's a sign of serratus anterior weakness.

🔍 Scapular Assistance Test (SAT)

If raising the arm hurts, have someone support the lower blade and help it rotate upward while you raise the arm again.

For reference: If the pain eases or the arm goes up more easily with help, it's strong evidence that faulty blade movement is part of the problem.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Manual therapy — pectoralis minor & fascia release

The therapist releases the pectoralis minor that pulls the blade forward and the stiff fascia around it, making room for the blade to return to its proper position.

🤲 Thoracic mobilization

If the upper back is stuck in a hunch, the blade can never move properly. The therapist restores upper-back joint motion to give the blade a flat 'floor.'

🤲 Kinesiology taping

Tape applied to guide the blade into the correct position helps your body remember the right position sense while you re-learn movement.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Scapular clock (waking up position sense)

With the shoulder relaxed, move only the shoulder blade slowly: up, down, together, apart. Just feeling where it moves is the goal.

⏱ 10 reps each direction × 2 sets, twice a day

🏃 Wall push-up plus (serratus strengthening)

From a wall push-up, after fully straightening the arms, push your back a hand-width rounder to spread the blades fully outward (the 'plus'). That final push is the heart of serratus work.

⏱ 10 reps × 3 sets, once a day (progress to knee push-ups)

🏃 Y-T-W raises (lower/mid trapezius)

Lying face down or bent forward, raise the arms into Y, T, and W shapes, holding 2 seconds each. Keep thumbs up and don't let the shoulders shrug.

⏱ 10 reps each shape × 2 sets, once a day

🏃 Pectoralis minor stretch (doorway stretch)

Place your forearm on a doorframe slightly above shoulder height and step forward to stretch the front of the chest.

⏱ 30 sec × 3 reps (each side), twice a day

🏃 Band rows (retraining the pulling pattern)

Anchor a band at chest height; squeeze the shoulder blades together first, then pull the elbows back. The order — 'blades first, arms second' — is the point. Lower the resistance if the shoulders shrug.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets, once a day

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • The shape of the blade suddenly changed after trauma (a fall, a blow)
  • Weakness and tingling in the arm (possible nerve problem)
  • Shoulder pain that doesn't improve at all for over 6 weeks of exercise
  • Shoulder pain that wakes you at night (suspect a rotator-cuff tear too)

⚠️ This material is for health information only and does not replace a doctor's diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms last more than 2 weeks or any red flag applies, see a medical professional.
