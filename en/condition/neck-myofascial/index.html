🦒 Neck

Myofascial Pain Syndrome (stiff/knotted neck)

Tight knots (trigger points) form inside the muscle; pressing them sends pain spreading to other areas. The most common kind of muscle pain.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

This is the medical name for what people call a 'crick' or a 'knotted muscle.' When part of an overworked muscle stays contracted and stiff, you can feel a hard 'trigger point' like a bean or a band.

Its hallmark is 'referred pain.' Pressing a trigger point in the upper shoulder can send pain up to the back of the head or temple — so the source and the painful spot can be different. That's why something you think is a headache is often the shoulder muscle to blame.

Why muscles knot up is simple: they were worked too long in one position (low-grade sustained contraction) or worked too hard suddenly. A muscle short on oxygen and nutrients from poor blood flow gets stiffer in a vicious cycle.

Why does it happen?

  • Holding one position for long periods (computer work, driving, phone)
  • Unconscious shoulder-shrugging from stress
  • Cold or a draft directly on the muscle, causing it to tense
  • Lack of sleep, a high pillow, sudden exercise or labor

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • A spot in the back of the neck or top of the shoulder that hurts sharply when pressed
  • Pressing that spot sends pain spreading to the head, temple, or shoulder blade
  • Pain is worse in the afternoon, after work, than in the morning
  • Turning the neck a certain way pulls and feels knotted
  • A hot pack or massage relieves it temporarily
  • No clear arm tingling (clear finger tingling suggests a disc instead)

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Finding the trigger point

Press firmly with a finger on the top of the shoulder (trapezius), the inner edge of the shoulder blade, and the side of the back of the neck.

For reference: If you feel a hard bean- or band-like spot and pressing it spreads pain to where you usually hurt (head, shoulder blade), it's myofascial pain syndrome.

🔍 Comparing neck range

Turn your head fully left and right, then tilt to each side.

For reference: If one direction turns noticeably less and feels tight, the muscle on the opposite side is likely knotted.

🔍 Telling it apart from a disc

Check whether there's clear tingling running into the arm or fingers, or whether coughing sends pain down the arm.

For reference: If there's none of that and the pain centers on the pressed spot, it's likely a muscle problem. If tingling is distinct, also check the neck disc page.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Manual therapy — ischemic compression & myofascial release

The therapist presses the trigger point steadily to release the stiff fibers (ischemic compression) and stretches the fascia wrapping the muscle. Pressing reproduces a 'good' pain with referred pain that then releases.

🤲 Shockwave (ESWT) / dry needling or injection (TPI)

Shockwave stimulates the stiff trigger point to boost blood flow, or a doctor uses a fine needle or injection to release the knot directly. Effective for old, stubborn trigger points.

🤲 Heat therapy

A hot pack or warm shower raises muscle temperature, widening vessels so oxygen reaches the stiff muscle. Doing it before exercise or stretching doubles the effect.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Self-massage with a ball

Trap a tennis or massage ball between a wall and the sore shoulder/shoulder blade, and press the trigger point steadily for 30–60 seconds. A 'good hurt' (about 5–6 out of 10) is right — don't press so hard you grimace.

⏱ 30–60 sec × 2–3 reps per spot, 1–2 times a day

🏃 Upper trapezius stretch

Hold under a chair with one hand to anchor the shoulder, and with the other hand pull your head diagonally to stretch the top of the shoulder. Hold smoothly without bouncing.

⏱ 30 sec × 3 reps (each side), 2–3 times a day

🏃 Shoulder shrug-and-drop (tension reset)

Inhale and shrug your shoulders hard up to your ears, hold 3 seconds, then exhale and let them drop. This resets the tension of shoulders raised by stress.

⏱ 10 reps, every 1–2 hours

🏃 Light aerobic exercise

Brisk walking or easy swimming to raise whole-body circulation. Because myofascial pain is fundamentally a blood-flow problem, aerobic exercise is itself treatment.

⏱ 30 minutes, 4–5 times a week

🏃 Shoulder-blade squeeze + downward set

Draw the shoulder blades toward the middle of your back and slightly down, holding 5 seconds. This wakes the muscles that stabilize the shoulder instead of the habitual shrug muscles.

⏱ 10 reps × 3 sets, twice a day

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • Clear tingling and electric sensations running into the arm or fingers (suspect a neck disc)
  • Fever with a stiff neck so you can't bend your head forward (suspect infection — emergency)
  • Pain that doesn't improve at all for over 4 weeks despite massage and heat
  • Night sweats and unexplained weight loss along with ongoing pain

⚠️ This material is for health information only and does not replace a doctor's diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms last more than 2 weeks or any red flag applies, see a medical professional.
