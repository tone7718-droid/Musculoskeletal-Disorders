🦿 Knee

Meniscus Tear (knee cartilage pad tear)

The shock-absorbing cartilage pad in the knee tears from twisting or wear, so the joint line hurts and the knee catches.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

Inside the knee, between the thigh and shin bones, sit two 'C'-shaped soft cartilage pads (menisci), one on the inner and one on the outer side. Like a car bumper, they absorb shock and stabilize the joint. A meniscus tear is when one of these pads rips.

There are two paths. The young tear it suddenly with a twisting injury on a bent knee (soccer, basketball, squatting-and-turning); after middle age, a worn, weakened meniscus tears from a trivial move — a 'degenerative tear,' often alongside knee osteoarthritis.

Recovery differs by location. The outer rim has blood supply and can heal, but the inner part has almost none and barely self-heals. Fortunately many cases — especially degenerative tears — improve without surgery through exercise therapy.

Why does it happen?

  • A twisting injury on a bent knee (sports, squatting-and-turning)
  • Age-related meniscus degeneration (tears from minor moves after middle age)
  • Repeated deep bending — squatting, sitting cross-legged
  • Accompanying knee osteoarthritis
  • Knee instability from a past ligament injury

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Pain along the knee joint line (the inner or outer gap)
  • Worse with squatting, stairs, and twisting moves
  • The knee 'catches' or locks so it won't straighten at a certain angle
  • Clicking or a sense of something pinching in the knee
  • Swelling after activity
  • The knee buckling weakly (giving way)

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Joint-line tenderness check

Sit with the knee bent 90° and press the joint gaps on either side of the front of the knee (the joint line).

For reference: Pinpoint pain along the inner or outer joint line makes a meniscus tear likely.

🔍 Squat test

Slowly squat down and up as far as comfortable (don't force it).

For reference: Reproduced joint-line pain or catching when squatting deep suggests meniscus irritation.

🔍 Locking/swelling check

Recall whether the knee truly locked (wouldn't straighten) or swells after activity.

For reference: True locking or repeated swelling may need precise imaging (MRI).

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Manual/physical therapy — mobilization & cued strengthening

The therapist restores knee movement and cues the thigh/hip muscles to protect the knee. For degenerative tears, much research shows exercise-based conservative care gives results similar to surgery.

🤲 Swelling/pain management

In the acute phase, ice, compression, elevation, and if needed doctor-prescribed medication calm swelling and pain so exercise can begin.

🤲 Surgical consult (selective)

Repeated true locking or a large tear (especially a repairable tear in the young) is considered for arthroscopy. Indiscriminate trimming of a degenerative tear isn't recommended — try exercise therapy first.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Front-thigh strengthening (pain-free range)

Straight-leg raises lying down and seated knee extension at pain-free angles. Avoid deep bending and twisting.

⏱ 10–15 reps × 3 sets, once a day

🏃 Glute strengthening (bridge, clamshell)

Building the glutes reduces the knee caving inward and protects the meniscus.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets each, once a day

🏃 Pain-free range-of-motion recovery

Heel slides and slowly bending/straightening the knee against a wall restore range without pain.

⏱ 10 reps × 2 sets, twice a day

🏃 Balance & function training (recovery phase)

Single-leg stands and mini-squats (pain-free depth) build knee control and a graded return to daily and sport movements.

⏱ 3 times a week, progressive

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • The knee locks and won't straighten (a caught cartilage fragment — see a doctor)
  • Rapid severe swelling right after trauma (rule out a ligament tear too)
  • The knee repeatedly buckles and gives way
  • A swollen, hot, red knee (rule out infection/inflammation)
  • Inability to bear weight
