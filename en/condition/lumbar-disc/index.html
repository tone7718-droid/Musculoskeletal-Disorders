🧍 Lower Back · Pelvis

Lumbar Disc Herniation

A disc between the lumbar bones bulges and presses a nerve heading to the leg, so the buttock and leg tingle and hurt more than the back.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The discs between the lumbar bones are cushions like jelly-filled donuts. Lifting heavy with a bent back or long slouched sitting concentrates pressure on the back of the disc; repeated, the outer ring tears and the inner jelly pushes backward.

When the bulging disc presses a nerve root running to the leg, you get sciatica. Depending on which nerve is pinched, the buttock, back of the thigh, calf, and foot tingle and hurt as if electric. Leg symptoms worse than the back itself are characteristic.

Important: most lumbar discs (about 90%) improve without surgery. The body's immune cells clean up the bulging fragment, and larger ruptures even tend to reabsorb better. The first 6–12 weeks are for conservative care.

Why does it happen?

  • Lifting with a bent back, or lifting while twisting
  • Long slouched sitting (disc pressure is higher sitting than standing)
  • Jobs/sports with repeated bending
  • Smoking (poor disc nutrition), obesity
  • Common in the 20s–40s (an age with enough 'jelly' to rupture)

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Electric pain/tingling from the buttock down the back of the thigh, calf, and foot
  • Leg symptoms more distressing than the back
  • Worse when sitting, better standing or walking
  • Cough, sneeze, or straining sends pain down the leg
  • Bending the back forward worsens the leg tingling
  • Dulled sensation or weakness in the toes/top of the foot

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Straight Leg Raise (SLR)

Lie on your back and slowly raise the affected straight leg (more accurate if someone lifts it).

For reference: If between 30–70° it reproduces tingling/pain down the thigh–calf–foot, nerve compression (a disc) is likely. Just an achy back is not positive.

🔍 Directional preference (McKenzie style)

Lie face down, prop on the elbows to extend the back, and hold 1–2 minutes, watching how the leg symptoms change.

For reference: If leg tingling climbs up toward the back and lessens (centralization), it's a good sign and extension exercises help. If it travels further down the leg, stop immediately.

🔍 Foot-strength check

Try walking on your heels, then on your tiptoes.

For reference: If you can't tiptoe or heel-walk on one side, nerve compression has weakened the muscles — see a doctor promptly.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Manual therapy — neural mobilization & spinal mobilization

The therapist glides the pinched nerve without adhesion by adjusting leg/back movement, and releases surrounding joints and muscles to ease the nerve pathway.

🤲 Traction

A machine gently pulls the back to briefly lower disc pressure. Effects vary by person, so it's used selectively for those whose leg symptoms ease during it.

🤲 Injections such as nerve block (by a doctor)

Anti-inflammatory medication around the nerve calms swelling and inflammation. A bridge to get through a severe acute phase and move on to exercise therapy.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Prone back extension (McKenzie extension)

Start propped on the elbows for 1–2 minutes, then progress to pushing up on the hands with the pelvis on the floor. Only for those whose leg symptoms eased in the self-observation.

⏱ 10 reps × 1 set, 4–6 times a day (every 2 hours)

🏃 Short, frequent walks

Walk often, as pain allows. Long sitting is the disc's enemy — after 30–40 minutes seated, stand and walk 2–3 minutes.

⏱ 10–20 min × 2–3 times a day + stand every 30 minutes

🏃 Nerve flossing (sciatic glide)

Sitting, extend the affected leg forward as you lift the head, then bend the leg as you drop the head. Glide the nerve gently like floss. Only within a range that doesn't worsen tingling.

⏱ 10 reps × 2 sets, twice a day

🏃 McGill Big 3 (curl-up, side plank, bird-dog)

Once the acute phase passes, start safe trunk strengthening that doesn't bend the back. Same as the non-specific back pain page.

⏱ 10 sec × 5–6 reps each, once a day

🏃 Hip-hinge for daily movements

Wash, pick things up, and lift a child by sending the hips back and folding at the hips instead of bending the back. Not re-opening the wound while the disc heals is the whole of recovery.

⏱ Lifestyle — always

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • Loss of bladder/bowel control or numb groin/anal area (cauda equina — may need emergency surgery)
  • Weak ankle or toes so the foot drags or you trip often
  • Both legs tingle and weaken at once
  • Daily life impossible from pain despite 6–12 weeks of conservative care
  • Excruciating pain that painkillers don't touch at all

⚠️ This material is for health information only and does not replace a doctor's diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms last more than 2 weeks or any red flag applies, see a medical professional.
