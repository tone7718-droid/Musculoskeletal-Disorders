Trigger Finger (Stenosing Tenosynovitis)

A nodule on a finger-flexing tendon catches on a pulley, so the finger locks bent and snaps straight with a click.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The finger-flexing tendons pass through several rings (pulleys) from the palm to the fingertip — like a fishing line through the guides on a rod. Overuse swells and thickens part of the tendon near the first pulley (where the palm meets the finger) into a small nodule.

Each time this nodule passes the pulley it catches and pops, creating the 'trigger' snap. Worse, the nodule can jam in the pulley so the finger locks bent and must be straightened with the other hand. Common in middle-aged women, people with diabetes, and hand-intensive jobs, especially the thumb, middle, and ring fingers.

Why does it happen?

  • Repeated gripping (tools, golf clubs, steering, scissors, phone)
  • Diabetes (higher risk, sometimes several fingers)
  • Rheumatoid arthritis, thyroid disease
  • More common in middle-aged women
  • Repeated direct pressure on the palm

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • In the morning the finger won't straighten easily from a bent position
  • Straightening the finger makes it snap open like a trigger
  • Tenderness and a pea-like nodule when you press the upper palm (below the finger-base joint)
  • A catching feeling when bending and straightening the finger
  • If severe, the finger locks bent and must be straightened with the other hand
  • Pain on the palm side when gripping

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Triggering check

Slowly make and open a fist 5 times, especially in the morning.

For reference: If only a certain finger catches and snaps when opening, it's trigger finger. The degree of catching (smooth → click → locked) tells you how severe it is.

🔍 Feeling the nodule

Press the base of the sore finger on the palm side while bending and straightening that finger.

For reference: A pea-like lump moving under your finger with pain confirms a tendon nodule.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Night extension splint

A splint holding the finger straight overnight stops the tendon from stiffening jammed in the pulley while you sleep, reducing morning locking. Effective in early cases.

🤲 Injection (by a doctor)

A steroid injection around the pulley calms tendon swelling. An effective first-line treatment for trigger finger, often resolving with 1–2 injections (less effective in diabetics).

🤲 Surgery (percutaneous/open pulley release)

If locking recurs or injections fail, a simple procedure opens the narrowed first pulley. Short and reliably effective.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Tendon gliding exercise

Move slowly through straight → hook → fist → flat fist so the tendon glides smoothly through the pulleys. Stay within a range without severe catching.

⏱ 5 cycles, 3–4 times a day

🏃 Finger spread-and-extend stretch

Place the palm on a table and gently spread the fingers fully straight, holding 10 seconds.

⏱ 10 sec × 5 reps, 2–3 times a day

🏃 Grip-strength control training

Practice using only 70% of the force needed to hold things. Death-grip habits (steering wheel, knife work, phone) are the main culprit of tendon swelling. Thicker-handled tools let you grip lighter.

⏱ Lifestyle — always

🏃 Warm soak + gentle massage

In the morning, soak the hand in warm water 5 minutes, gently rub around the palm nodule, then do gliding exercises to reduce morning catching.

⏱ 5–10 minutes each morning

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • The finger locks fully bent and won't straighten
  • Several fingers affected at once (test for diabetes/rheumatoid)
  • Swollen joints with morning stiffness over an hour (rule out rheumatoid)
  • Repeated recurrence after 2 injections (consider surgery)
