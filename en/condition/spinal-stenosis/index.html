Lumbar Spinal Stenosis

The spinal tunnel for the nerves narrows with age, so the legs tingle when walking and you must stop and rest.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

Down the center of the spine runs a tunnel (the spinal canal) carrying the nerve bundle. With age, discs flatten and bulge, and joints and ligaments thicken, gradually narrowing this tunnel. The squeezed nerves get poor blood supply, so when put to work (walking) they tire and tingle quickly.

The hallmark is 'neurogenic claudication.' Walking makes the legs tingle and feel bursting until you stop and rest, and bending forward or sitting curiously relieves it fast — because bending widens the tunnel. So a stenosis patient can walk longer pushing a cart (a slightly bent posture).

It mainly appears after age 60 as a degenerative change and progresses slowly. Many manage well with exercise and care; surgery is an option only when quality of life drops badly.

Why does it happen?

  • Age-related degeneration (disc bulge, joint/ligament thickening)
  • A congenitally narrow canal
  • Accompanying spondylolisthesis (a vertebra slipping forward)
  • Years of accumulated back load

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Walking makes the buttocks/legs tingle and feel heavy so you must stop and rest
  • Bending forward or sitting quickly relieves the leg tingling
  • You can walk longer pushing a stroller or cart
  • Downhill (back extended) is harder than uphill
  • Just standing makes the legs tingle
  • Leg symptoms often in both legs
  • The distance you can walk has shrunk over months to years

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Walk-rest pattern check

Note how far you can walk continuously and what posture relieves it when you stop.

For reference: Stopping for leg tingling every so often, relieved only by 'sitting or bending,' is the stenosis pattern. (Vascular claudication eases just by standing still — a key difference.)

🔍 Bike vs. walk comparison

Compare how long you can go without leg tingling on a stationary bike (a bent-forward posture) versus walking.

For reference: If you can bike a long time but only walking is hard, stenosis is likely. If both are equally hard, vascular testing is also needed.

🔍 Back-extension test

Standing, slowly bend the back backward and hold about 30 seconds.

For reference: If the extended posture triggers leg tingling, suspect stenosis. Stop as soon as symptoms appear.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Manual therapy — flexion-direction mobilization & release

The therapist gently moves the spine in flexion and releases stiff hip and buttock muscles to lighten the nerve pathway. Loosening the hips lets you walk with less back extension.

🤲 Injection (by a doctor)

An epidural injection around the nerve calms inflammation and swelling to lengthen walking distance. Duration varies by person and it's most valuable combined with exercise.

🤲 Walking aids

A walker, trekking poles, or a cart create a slightly bent posture — both 'treatment' and an 'activity-keeping device.' Walking with an aid is far better than not walking.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Knee-to-chest stretch (flexion exercise)

Lie down and hug both knees to the chest, holding 30 seconds. A movement that widens the narrowed tunnel — the basic stenosis exercise.

⏱ 30 sec × 5 reps, 2–3 times a day

🏃 Stationary cycling

The slightly bent posture lets you do aerobic work without leg tingling. The best aerobic exercise for stenosis patients who find walking hard.

⏱ 20–30 min, 4–5 times a week

🏃 Interval walking

Sit and rest 'before' the tingling comes, then repeat. E.g. if tingling starts at 200 m, sit 1–2 minutes every 150 m. The goal is to keep total walking volume.

⏱ About 20–30 min of total walking, daily

🏃 Posterior pelvic tilt

Lie with knees bent and gently press the back into the floor, lifting the tailbone slightly, holding 5 seconds. A trunk-control move to reduce over-extension.

⏱ 5 sec × 10 reps, twice a day

🏃 Hip-flexor (iliopsoas) stretch

In a half-kneeling lunge, push the pelvis forward to stretch the front of the groin. Brace the abs so the back doesn't extend. Stiff hip-fronts make you extend the back more when walking, worsening symptoms.

⏱ 30 sec × 3 reps (each side), 1–2 times a day

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • Bladder/bowel changes, numb perineum (emergency)
  • Rapidly weakening legs so the foot drags
  • Leg pain that doesn't ease with rest and persists day and night
  • Walking distance dropping sharply over weeks
  • Daily life (errands, shopping) becoming impossible despite treatment (consider surgery consult)

⚠️ This material is for health information only and does not replace a doctor's diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms last more than 2 weeks or any red flag applies, see a medical professional.
