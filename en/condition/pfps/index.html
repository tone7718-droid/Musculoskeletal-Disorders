🦿 Knee

Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome (runner's knee / front-knee pain)

The kneecap doesn't glide smoothly in its groove on the thigh bone, so stairs and squatting hurt the front of the knee.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The round bone on the front of the knee (the patella) rides up and down a groove on the thigh bone like a train on a rail. Each time you bend and straighten the knee, this 'train' slides on its 'rail.' If muscle imbalance in the thigh/hip or foot alignment pulls the train slightly off the rail, pressure builds on the cartilage behind the kneecap and the front of the knee hurts.

Common in young, active people, especially women and runners — hence 'runner's knee.' X-rays usually show nothing, so you hear 'the bone is fine,' but the pain is real — a classic 'movement problem.' That's why the answer lies in exercise, not injections.

An important clue: the root often isn't the knee but above it (weak hip muscles) or below it (foot alignment).

Why does it happen?

  • A sudden increase in running, hiking, or stairs
  • Weak outer-hip muscles (gluteus medius) letting the knee cave inward
  • Thigh-muscle imbalance and poor flexibility
  • Flat feet / overpronation and other foot-alignment issues
  • Long sitting with the knee bent (the 'movie-theater sign')

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Broad pain around the front of the knee, around the kneecap (hard to pinpoint)
  • Especially painful going down stairs (more than up)
  • Pain squatting, sitting cross-legged, or long driving
  • Long sitting (theater, plane) makes you want to straighten the leg
  • A grinding feeling or sound in the knee
  • Aching at the front of the knee late in a run or the next day

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Stair-descent test

Walk slowly down stairs and watch for front-knee pain.

For reference: Pain going down but less going up is the classic patellofemoral pattern.

🔍 Single-leg mini-squat alignment watch

In front of a mirror, stand on the affected leg and do 5 small squats. Watch where the knee points.

For reference: If the knee caves inward past the big toe (knee-in), weak hip muscles are likely the root.

🔍 Long-sitting test (movie-theater sign)

Note how it feels to sit with the knee bent 90° for over 30 minutes.

For reference: If the front of the knee aches and you want to straighten the leg, it suggests increased pressure behind the kneecap.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Manual therapy — patellar mobilization & fascia release

The therapist gently mobilizes the kneecap to release surrounding tightness and frees the stiff outer-thigh fascia and calf, creating a good environment for the kneecap to track properly.

🤲 Taping (McConnell taping)

Taping that nudges the kneecap slightly inward often reduces pain immediately during exercise — a helpful aid for exercising pain-free.

🤲 Foot orthotics (if needed)

If flat feet/overpronation are marked, insoles can reduce the twisting that travels up to the knee.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Outer-hip strengthening (clamshell → side plank)

Start with clamshells, then progress to side planks and banded side-walks as you get used to it. The muscles that keep the knee from caving in are the #1 PFPS rehab exercise.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets, once a day

🏃 Spanish squat (knee-friendly thigh exercise)

Loop a thick band behind the knees to a post, lean back, and squat with the torso upright. Strengthens the thighs at an angle with less kneecap pressure.

⏱ 10 reps × 3 sets, 3–4 times a week

🏃 Step-down (alignment practice)

Stand on the affected leg on a low step, keep the knee pointing over the second toe, tap the other heel lightly to the floor, and come back up. Check alignment in a mirror.

⏱ 10 reps × 3 sets, once a day

🏃 Front/outer-thigh foam roll + stretch

Gently foam-roll the front and outside of the thigh, and stand holding the foot to stretch the front of the thigh 30 seconds.

⏱ Foam roll 1–2 min + stretch 30 sec × 3 reps, once a day

🏃 Adjusting running volume + cadence (runners)

Cut distance so pain stays under 3, then add under 10% a week. Shortening stride and raising step rate 5–10% reduces knee load.

⏱ Apply the 10% weekly rule

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • A swollen knee with fluid (rule out an intra-joint problem)
  • A feeling of the kneecap slipping/dislocating or the knee buckling (kneecap dislocation/ligament issue)
  • Pain that started after trauma
  • Episodes of locking so it won't straighten (rule out a meniscus tear)
  • No improvement after 3 months of exercise therapy

⚠️ This material is for health information only and does not replace a doctor's diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms last more than 2 weeks or any red flag applies, see a medical professional.
