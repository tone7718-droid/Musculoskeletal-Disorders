Adductor (Groin) Strain

The inner-thigh adductor muscles stretch or tear with sudden cutting or kicking, so the groin hurts.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The inner-thigh adductors pull the leg inward and work strongly when changing direction or kicking a ball. When you suddenly cut in soccer, kick with the inside of the foot, or the legs split apart on ice, the muscle braces while lengthening — and in that instant the fibers can tear. This is an adductor strain.

Along with hamstring injuries, it's one of the most common muscle injuries in sports. Pain starts in the inner groin and spreads down the inner thigh, hurting when you bring the leg in or kick. Severity ranges from a mild stretch to a partial tear.

It's a recurrence-prone injury, so returning as soon as pain fades re-injures easily. Progressive strengthening, especially slowly regaining the adduction force, matters.

Why does it happen?

  • Sudden direction change, acceleration, deceleration (soccer, basketball, hockey, tennis)
  • Repeated/overloaded inside-foot kicking
  • A slip that suddenly splits the legs apart
  • Poor warm-up, weak/inflexible adductors
  • A history of groin injury, poor core/pelvic stability

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Pain in the inner groin to the inner thigh
  • Worse when bringing the leg inward or kicking a ball
  • A 'twinge' at the moment of injury, then limping
  • A tender spot in the inner thigh, sometimes with bruising
  • Reproduced pain with running, jumping, cutting
  • If severe, groin tightness even with walking and stairs

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Resisted adduction test

Lie down with a fist or ball between the knees and slowly squeeze (within pain 3).

For reference: Reproduced familiar pain in the inner groin when squeezing makes an adductor injury likely.

🔍 Tender-spot check

Press along where the adductors attach to the bone in the inner groin and the inner-thigh muscle.

For reference: A clear tender spot (or bruising) suggests injury there.

🔍 Estimating severity

Check whether you can walk/bear weight and how much adduction strength is lost.

For reference: Limping but walking suggests mild–moderate; difficulty walking with large bruising or a felt divot suggests severe — see a doctor.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Acute care (protect–compress–elevate)

For the first days, reduce hard activity and manage swelling with compression/elevation. Use ice briefly for pain only.

🤲 Manual therapy — soft-tissue work & pelvic alignment

In recovery, the therapist uses gentle massage so the scar heals supple, and balances pelvic/hip alignment and surrounding muscles to lower re-injury risk.

🤲 Shockwave (chronic attachment pain)

For old adductor-attachment pain (chronic groin pain), shockwave is sometimes used to stimulate healing.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Stage 1 — pain-free isometric adduction

Lie with knees bent and a ball between the knees, squeezing gently for 5–10 seconds. Starting early within pain 3 speeds recovery.

⏱ 10 sec × 10 reps, 1–2 times a day

🏃 Stage 2 — Copenhagen plank (easy version)

Lie on your side with the top leg on a chair/sofa and lift the pelvis using that leg. Start short and build up. A core exercise for adductor prevention/rehab.

⏱ 10–20 sec × 3–5 reps (each side), every other day

🏃 Stage 3 — adduction strengthening (band, side lunge)

Pull the leg inward against a band, and do side lunges that step out and pull back in, progressively loading.

⏱ 12 reps × 3 sets, 3 times a week

🏃 Stage 4 — return to cutting / sport

Straight running → curves → zig-zag → accel/decel → kicking, advancing only when there's no pain and no next-day reaction.

⏱ At least several days per stage, progressive

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • A 'pop' with severe swelling/bruising making walking hard (severe tear/avulsion — see a doctor)
  • A lump/bulge in the groin that hurts with cough (rule out a hernia)
  • Pain spreading to the lower abdomen/testicle or urinary symptoms (rule out other causes)
  • Groin pain at rest and at night (rule out a hip/bone problem)
  • An adolescent with groin pain unable to bear weight (rule out a growth-plate avulsion)
