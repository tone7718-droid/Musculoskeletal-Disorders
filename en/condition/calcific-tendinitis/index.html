🏋️ Shoulder

Calcific Tendinitis

Chalk-like calcium deposits build up in a shoulder tendon and cause severe pain as the body dissolves them.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

Calcium deposits collect inside the rotator-cuff tendon, forming a lump like toothpaste or chalk dust. Why it happens isn't fully clear, but it's thought cell changes in a poorly-supplied area of the tendon let calcium accumulate. It's especially common in women aged 30–60.

Interestingly, the worst pain comes not when calcium is 'building up' but when the body is 'dissolving and reabsorbing' it. In the resorptive phase the inflammatory reaction explodes — people sometimes end up in the ER with a shoulder that suddenly feels broken at midnight, despite no injury. Conversely, many people have deposits and never know it.

Most cases end well as the calcium is reabsorbed over time. The goal of treatment is to reduce the pain of that process and keep the shoulder from stiffening.

Why does it happen?

  • Exact cause unknown (poor tendon blood flow and cell change are leading theories)
  • More common in ages 30–60 and in women
  • Higher risk with diabetes or thyroid disease
  • Repetitive shoulder use may contribute
  • Usually occurs unrelated to trauma

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Sudden, almost 'breaking' shoulder pain with no injury (acute resorptive phase)
  • Pain so severe you can't move the arm at all, even to dress
  • Excruciating night pain that prevents sleep
  • Wincing pain from even a light touch on the front/outer shoulder
  • (Chronic form) catching pain at a certain angle lasting months
  • A memory of similar pain that came and went on its own before

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 History check

Note whether there was trauma, how fast the pain came on (worst within hours to a day?), and any past similar episodes.

For reference: Shoulder pain that became excruciating within a day or two with no injury strongly suggests the acute resorptive phase. Here an X-ray at the clinic (calcium shows well on X-ray) is the fastest, most accurate check.

🔍 Painful arc test (chronic form)

If the pain is the bearable, chronic kind, slowly raise the arm out to the side.

For reference: Catching pain in a certain band near shoulder height suggests a chronic form where calcium causes impingement (like impingement syndrome).

🔍 Telling it apart from frozen shoulder

When pain is less, have someone slowly raise your arm and check whether it goes all the way up.

For reference: If passive range is near normal once you avoid the acute pain, it leans toward calcific tendinitis; if everything is firmly stiff, toward frozen shoulder.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Shockwave (ESWT)

The signature non-surgical treatment — shockwaves break the deposit into small pieces and promote reabsorption. Effective in the chronic form, usually once a week for several sessions.

🤲 Ultrasound-guided calcium aspiration (by a doctor)

Using ultrasound to see the deposit, a needle washes and suctions it out. Gives fast relief when acute pain is severe or the deposit is large.

🤲 Medication / injection (by a doctor)

For the excruciating pain of the resorptive phase, anti-inflammatories and a bursa steroid injection quickly put out the fire. Toughing it out is not a virtue here.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Acute phase — pendulum only

In the excruciating phase, no forceful exercise. Use the pendulum as much as pain allows just to keep the shoulder from stiffening. A sling for short comfort is fine, but avoid full immobilization longer than 3–4 days.

⏱ 1 minute at a time, 3–4 times a day

🏃 After pain eases — stick-assisted range of motion

Use a stick to restore forward elevation and external rotation within pain 3. The aim is to quickly regain range lost during the acute phase.

⏱ 10 reps each direction, 2–3 times a day

🏃 Band external/internal rotation (cuff strengthening)

Once pain settles, strengthen the cuff to build the tendon's blood flow and load capacity. Keep the elbow at your side and go slowly.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets, once a day

🏃 Scapular stabilization + posture correction

Wall push-up plus and chest stretches create alignment where the tendon isn't pinched. Key to preventing recurrence.

⏱ 10 reps each × 2 sets, once a day

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • Fever with a red, swollen shoulder (must rule out septic arthritis — emergency)
  • Pain not controlled at all even with painkillers
  • No strength in the arm even after pain subsides (suspect a tear too)
  • The shoulder keeps stiffening over weeks (frozen shoulder developing)
