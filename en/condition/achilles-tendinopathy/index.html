Achilles Tendinopathy

The body's thickest tendon wears from overload, so above the heel hurts and stiffens.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The Achilles tendon connects the calf muscles to the heel bone — the thickest, strongest tendon in the body. It's a steel spring bearing several times body weight with every step, jump, and walk, but when load keeps exceeding healing, the collagen fibers inside become disordered and worn — thicker, stiffer, and painful.

There are two types by location. The 'mid-portion' type (2–6 cm above the heel) is common; the 'insertional' type (where the tendon attaches to the bone) needs slightly different rehab (a heel-drop stretch off a step can be harmful for the insertional type).

Common in runners who increased mileage suddenly and weekend-warrior middle-aged men. Following the shared tendon rule, the answer is not complete rest but 'training with appropriate load.'

Why does it happen?

  • A sudden increase in running distance or hill training
  • Weak calves plus sudden exercise (the weekend-warrior pattern)
  • Stiff calves, foot overpronation
  • Cushion/heel changes (suddenly switching to flats or barefoot running)
  • Age (tendon elasticity drops after the 30s), a history of quinolone antibiotics

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Pain and tenderness at the tendon above the heel
  • Stiff, painful tendon on the first morning steps
  • Pain at the start of exercise that eases as you warm up and returns after
  • The tendon feels thicker than the other side
  • Worse with stairs, uphills, and running
  • If severe, pain even with flat walking

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Tenderness location check

Pinch the Achilles between thumb and index finger and press from bottom to top.

For reference: Pain 2–6 cm above the heel bone is the mid-portion type; pain where it attaches to the bone is the insertional type. Rehab differs by type, so note the location.

🔍 Single-leg heel-raise test

Do as many single-leg heel raises as you can on each side and compare.

For reference: Noticeably fewer on the affected side, or pain stopping you, confirms reduced tendon/calf function. Use it to track recovery.

🔍 Rupture check (Thompson test)

Lie face down with the knee bent 90° and have someone squeeze the middle of the calf.

For reference: Normally the foot flicks down. If the foot doesn't move at all, suspect an Achilles rupture — go to a hospital immediately.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Shockwave (ESWT)

A proven adjunct for chronic Achilles tendinopathy, stimulating healing of the worn tendon. Works well combined with exercise.

🤲 Manual therapy — calf release & ankle mobilization

The therapist releases the stiff calf and restores ankle motion to reduce the load on the tendon.

🤲 Heel lift

A ~1 cm pad in the shoe heel reduces the pull on the tendon and eases daily pain. A useful temporary strategy, especially for the insertional type.

🤲 Avoid steroid injections

Steroid injections in or around the Achilles raise rupture risk and are generally not recommended.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Stage 1 — isometric heel-raise holds

Rise onto both heels and hold at the top 30–45 seconds. A safe start that loads the tendon while calming pain.

⏱ 45 sec × 5 reps, 1–2 times a day

🏃 Stage 2 — slow heel raises (two-leg → single-leg)

Do heel raises at 3 seconds up, 3 seconds down. Progress to single-leg, then add a backpack with books for weight. Do both knee-straight and knee-bent versions.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets, every other day

🏃 Stage 3 — eccentric step heel-drop (mid-portion type only!)

Stand on a step's edge on the forefoot, rise on both legs, then lower the heel below the step over 3 seconds on the affected leg only. ※ For the insertional type, don't drop below step level — go only to floor level.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets (knee straight/bent), once a day

🏃 Stage 4 — elastic recovery (jump training)

Light stationary skips → two-foot jumps → single-leg jumps to restore the tendon's spring. Start after stages 2–3 are pain-free.

⏱ 20–30 reps × 2 sets each, 2–3 times a week

🏃 Return to running + calf-stretch routine

Return on flat, short distances every other day, increasing under 10% a week. Make post-exercise calf stretches (knee straight/bent, 30 sec each) a routine.

⏱ The 10% rule + stretch daily

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • A kicked feeling and 'pop' during exercise after which you can't stand on tiptoe (rupture — go to a hospital immediately)
  • The foot doesn't move in the Thompson test
  • The tendon area suddenly very swollen and red
  • Tendon pain that started while on quinolone antibiotics (ask a doctor)
  • No improvement after 6 months of rehab
