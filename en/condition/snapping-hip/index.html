Snapping Hip Syndrome

A tendon flicks over a bony point as the hip moves, causing a 'snap/click' and a catching feeling.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

A 'snap' you feel or hear when raising or rotating the leg is snapping hip. Most often it's a thick tendon flicking over a bony prominence — harmless in itself.

There are two kinds. The lateral type: the IT band on the outer hip flicks over the greater trochanter, snapping when you walk or lie on your side. The internal type: the iliopsoas tendon at the front of the hip flicks over the pelvic bone, clicking at the front groin. Common in dancers, runners, and repeated-flexion sports.

If it only snaps without pain, usually no treatment is needed. But if pain comes with the snap, the tendon/bursa has accumulated inflammation, managed with flexibility and strength. Rarely, an internal joint (labrum) cause needs separate evaluation.

Why does it happen?

  • Tightness/shortening of the IT band or iliopsoas tendon
  • Repeated hip flexion/rotation (dance, running, soccer, yoga)
  • A sudden increase in activity
  • Poor pelvis/core stability concentrating load on the tendon
  • Either extreme of flexibility (too much or too little)

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • A 'snap/click' and catching in the hip when raising or rotating the leg
  • (Lateral) snapping on the outer hip and discomfort lying on the side
  • (Internal) clicking at the front groin and catching when straightening the leg
  • Repeats with stairs, running, or certain moves
  • Sometimes pain or aching with the snap
  • Worse after long sitting then standing, or after exercise

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Movement reproduction (lateral type)

Standing, swing the leg front-to-back, or lie on your side and raise/lower the top leg.

For reference: A 'snap' flicking over the outer hip suggests the lateral (IT band) type.

🔍 Movement reproduction (internal type)

Lie down, bend and abduct the hip (like a figure-4), then slowly straighten the leg.

For reference: Clicking and catching at the front groin suggests the internal (iliopsoas) type.

🔍 Pain-with-snap check

Note whether it only snaps or whether pain/swelling come with it.

For reference: Snapping with no pain is usually reassuring. Snapping with pain/locking needs evaluation for tendon inflammation or an intra-joint problem.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Manual therapy — IT band/iliopsoas release

The therapist releases the snapping tendon and its surrounding fascia to lower tension, and adjusts pelvic alignment so the tendon passes the bone smoothly.

🤲 Inflammation management (if painful)

If the tendon/bursa is inflamed, ice and, if needed, doctor-prescribed anti-inflammatories calm it. For severe pain, an ultrasound-guided injection is sometimes considered.

🤲 Activity/movement review

Dancers and runners review posture and movement patterns to correct factors that repeatedly load the tendon.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 IT band / TFL release

Foam-roll the outer thigh and outer hip gently, and stand crossing the leg back/side to stretch the outer hip.

⏱ Release 1–2 min + stretch 30 sec × 3 reps, once a day

🏃 Iliopsoas (hip-front) stretch

In a lunge, push the pelvis forward to stretch the front groin. Brace the abs so the back doesn't extend.

⏱ 30 sec × 3 reps (each side), twice a day

🏃 Outer-hip strengthening (clamshell, side leg raise)

Strengthening the gluteus medius stabilizes the pelvis, reducing the load on the tendon so the snapping eases.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets, once a day

🏃 Core + hip control (dead bug)

Move the legs slowly while keeping the trunk stable, creating smooth movement so the tendon doesn't snap suddenly.

⏱ 10 reps × 3 sets (each side), once a day

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • Snapping with deep groin pain, locking, or pinching (rule out an intra-joint problem like a labral tear)
  • Difficulty bearing weight or a limp
  • Sudden onset after trauma
  • Pain/swelling lasting over 6 weeks despite exercise management
