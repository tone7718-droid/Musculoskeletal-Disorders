🧍 Lower Back · Pelvis

Spondylolisthesis (a slipped vertebra)

An upper vertebra slips forward over the one below, causing back pain and sometimes leg symptoms.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

The vertebrae should stack neatly like blocks. In spondylolisthesis, one upper vertebra slides forward over the one below, out of alignment — usually in the lower back (L4–L5, L5–S1).

Two causes are common. In adolescents/athletes, a stress fracture at the bony bridge (the pars) lets it slip — the 'isthmic' type. After middle age, worn discs and joints become unstable and slip — the 'degenerative' type, which can come with spinal stenosis and leg tingling.

If the slip isn't severe, most are managed without surgery. The key is not to over-extend the back and to build a firm trunk (core) to stabilize the misaligned segment.

Why does it happen?

  • A stress fracture of the pars (back-extending sports — gymnastics, weightlifting, soccer; in adolescence)
  • Age-related disc/facet degeneration causing segmental instability (degenerative type)
  • Repeatedly over-extending the back
  • Weak core strength
  • Genetic/structural predisposition

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Back pain when standing long or extending the back
  • Bending forward actually feels better
  • Worse with activity, better with rest
  • Aching and a sense of instability (a 'giving' feeling) in the lower back
  • (If present) tingling down the buttock and leg
  • Pressing a certain spine segment feels a step or hurts

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Extension vs. flexion comparison

Standing, slowly bend the back backward, then forward.

For reference: Pain on extension and comfort on flexion fits spondylolisthesis/pars problems (a disc is usually the opposite).

🔍 Single-leg extension test

Stand on one leg (safely beside a wall) and gently extend the back. Both sides.

For reference: Reproduced back pain extending to one side suggests pars stress. Stop if pain appears.

🔍 Leg-symptom check

Note whether tingling/pain goes below the knee, or whether walking makes the legs tingle so you must rest.

For reference: Leg tingling or neurogenic claudication suggests accompanying stenosis and needs precise evaluation.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Manual therapy — flexion-based mobilization & release

The therapist gently mobilizes in the pain-easing direction (usually flexion) and releases the over-tense back extensors and hip-front muscles to relieve the misaligned segment. Maneuvers that force extension are avoided.

🤲 Brace / taping

In an acute flare or with heavy activity, a back brace can briefly protect the segment. A temporary support until strength returns.

🤲 Medication / injection (by a doctor)

If pain/inflammation is severe or there are leg nerve symptoms, medication or a nerve injection controls it — to create a state where you can start exercising.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Posterior pelvic tilt + abdominal bracing

Lie with knees bent, press the back gently into the floor, and brace the lower abdomen. The basic move to reduce over-extension and switch the core on.

⏱ 5 sec × 10 reps × 2 sets, twice a day

🏃 Dead bug (flexion stability)

Lie with arms and legs up and slowly lower the opposite arm/leg without letting the back lift off the floor. Learn to use the trunk without extending the back.

⏱ 10 reps × 3 sets (each side), once a day

🏃 Glute bridge & bird-dog (neutral)

Strengthen the glutes and trunk while keeping a 'neutral' back, not over-extended. Weak glutes make the back extend instead.

⏱ 10–12 reps × 3 sets each, once a day

🏃 Hip-flexor (iliopsoas) stretch

In a lunge, brace the abs and push the pelvis forward to stretch the front of the groin. Be careful not to extend the back.

⏱ 30 sec × 3 reps (each side), once a day

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • Bladder/bowel changes or numb perineum (cauda equina — emergency)
  • Rapidly weakening legs or a dragging foot
  • Worsening leg tingling and a sharply shrinking walking distance
  • Pain that doesn't ease at rest or at night
  • An adolescent with back pain lasting over 2 weeks (rule out a pars stress fracture)

⚠️ This material is for health information only and does not replace a doctor's diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms last more than 2 weeks or any red flag applies, see a medical professional.
