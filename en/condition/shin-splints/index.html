Shin Splints (Medial Tibial Stress Syndrome)

Repeated impact from running and jumping irritates the inner edge of the shin bone and nearby tissue, so the shin aches with each stride.

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

A regular for new runners, military recruits, and athletes early in the season. Each stride loads the shin bone with several times body weight; bone normally remodels stronger under load. The problem is when load piles up faster than remodeling — the bone surface, the membrane around it, and muscle attachments get irritated and the inner shin aches broadly.

What matters is 'telling it apart from a stress fracture.' Shin splints hurt broadly over a palm-width of the inner shin; a stress fracture hurts at a coin-sized 'single point' and worsens until even walking hurts. Ignoring shin splints and running on can progress to a stress fracture, so read this signal carefully.

Why does it happen?

  • A sudden increase in running distance or intensity (the most common cause)
  • Hard surfaces, worn-out shoes
  • Overpronated gait, flat feet
  • Weak, inflexible calf/shin muscles
  • Starting high-impact exercise while overweight

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Broad aching along the inner edge of the shin (centered on the lower third)
  • Pain at the start of a run that may ease, then worse and constant if it progresses
  • Aching after exercise or the next morning
  • A broad area hurts when you run a finger down the inner shin
  • Pain even with jumping or brisk walking
  • Eases with rest, returns when you run again

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Tenderness-area check (stress-fracture rule-out 1)

Press down the inner edge of the shin bone with a finger, top to bottom, and note the painful range.

For reference: Broad pain over 5+ cm suggests shin splints; a coin-sized single point that's especially painful suggests a stress fracture — don't run, see a doctor.

🔍 Single-leg hop test (stress-fracture rule-out 2)

Hop lightly on the affected leg 5–10 times.

For reference: A drilling pain at one point with each hop suggests a stress fracture. Broad, dull pain leans toward shin splints.

🔍 Training-volume review

Look back at your training the past 2–4 weeks.

For reference: If distance, frequency, or intensity jumped over 30% suddenly, that's the likely cause. Knowing the cause makes the prescription (managing volume) clear.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Manual therapy — calf/shin muscle release

The therapist releases the muscles attaching to the inner shin (tibialis posterior, soleus) and the calf to reduce the pull at the bone attachment.

🤲 Shock absorption — shoe/insole check

Switch to running shoes with intact cushioning and, if overpronation is marked, use arch-supporting insoles. Check shoe mileage (usually 500–800 km).

🤲 Ice massage

Ice the sore area 10–15 minutes after exercise to calm pain and irritation. For symptom control — the root treatment is load management and strength.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Load management (half the treatment)

Rather than stopping running entirely, cut distance/frequency to keep pain under 3 and keep fitness with cycling/swimming. When pain settles to 0–2, add under 10% a week.

⏱ Adjust weekly by pain score

🏃 Calf raises (calf & tibialis posterior strengthening)

Do calf raises 3 seconds up, 3 seconds down. Loading slightly toward the inner foot makes the inner-shin muscles work more. Progress to single-leg for difficulty.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets, every other day

🏃 Toe raises (front-shin muscle strengthening)

Stand with your back against a wall and lift the front of the foot up and down.

⏱ 15 reps × 3 sets, every other day

🏃 Single-leg balance + foot-arch exercise

Build the foot's shock absorption with single-leg stands and toe-scrunching a towel.

⏱ 1–2 min each, daily

🏃 Running-form fix — raise cadence

Raise step rate 5–10% to shorten stride, spreading the landing impact and reducing shin load. Use a metronome app or watch.

⏱ Apply every run

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • A coin-sized single point that worsens and hurts even walking (stress fracture — see a doctor)
  • Pain at rest and at night
  • The shin bursting-tight and squeezing during exercise with foot tingling (rule out chronic compartment syndrome)
  • One calf swollen and warm (rule out a blood clot)
