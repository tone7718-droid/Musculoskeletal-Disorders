Ankle Sprain / Chronic Ankle Instability

The ankle rolls inward and the outer ligaments stretch or tear — the most common sports injury. Heal it carelessly and it becomes a 'frequently spraining ankle.'

✨ Open the interactive version (symptom check · compare)

📖 What is this condition?

About 90% of ankle sprains happen when the sole rolls inward (inversion), injuring the ligaments around the outer ankle bone. Ligaments are tough straps connecting bone to bone — a slight stretch is grade 1, a partial tear grade 2, a complete tear grade 3.

The real danger of an ankle sprain is the 'aftermath.' Ligaments are full of sensors that report the ankle's position to the brain (proprioception), and these get damaged with the ligament. If the ligament heals but you don't retrain the sensors, the brain is late to sense the ankle's position and you re-injure it — 'chronic ankle instability.' Many first-time sprainers go down this road.

So the key to ankle rehab is not 'done when the swelling's gone' but 'done after balance training too.'

Why does it happen?

  • Landing on someone's foot or uneven ground after a jump (basketball, volleyball, soccer)
  • Missteps on stairs/curbs, walking in high heels
  • Inadequate rehab after a past sprain (the top cause of recurrence)
  • Weak ankle muscles and poor balance
  • Hypermobile joints (naturally loose ligaments)

✅ Do you have these symptoms?

  • Swelling and bruising around the outer ankle bone right after the sprain
  • Pain on the outer ankle when bearing weight
  • (Chronic) the ankle keeps feeling like it will roll on uneven or dark ground
  • (Chronic) spraining the same ankle several times a year
  • (Chronic) swelling and aching after long walks or exercise
  • (Chronic) the ankle wobbles when standing on one leg

🔬 Self-observations you can do at home

Go slowly and stay within a pain-free range. These responses are observations, not a diagnosis.

🔍 Ottawa ankle rules (do I need an X-ray?)

Right after the sprain: ① press the back-lower edge of each ankle bone (6 cm) for tenderness; ② check whether you could take four steps right after and now.

For reference: If there's bony tenderness behind the ankle bone or you can't take four steps, a fracture is possible — go for an X-ray. If neither, fracture is unlikely.

🔍 Single-leg balance test (recovery/chronic)

On flat ground, stand on one leg for 30 seconds, then eyes closed for 10 seconds if you can. Compare both sides.

For reference: If the injured side wobbles a lot or collapses fast with eyes closed, the balance sensors haven't recovered — a sign of high re-injury risk.

🔍 Instability questionnaire

Ask yourself: going down stairs, on uneven ground, during exercise — 'can I trust this ankle?'

For reference: If you 'don't trust it and have to be careful,' chronic instability is developing. Even without pain, balance/strength rehab is needed.

🤲 Hands-on care — provided by a professional

🤲 Acute care (protect–compress–elevate)

For the first 2–3 days, manage swelling with a compression wrap and elevation, and protect with a short-term brace for severe pain. But 'moving early within a pain-free range' heals faster than long immobilization (like a cast) — the current standard.

🤲 Manual therapy — joint mobilization

Once swelling subsides, the therapist restores ankle glide (especially the talus) so dorsiflexion returns to normal. Walking with limited dorsiflexion makes other areas compensate and create new pain.

🤲 Taping / brace

Early in return to sport, taping or a brace reduces re-injury. Not a lifelong crutch — a safety belt until balance recovers.

🏃 Active care — exercises you do yourself

🏃 Stage 1 — ankle alphabet & pumps (from right after)

Sit or lie and 'write' the alphabet in the air with the toes, and pump the ankle up and down. Reduces swelling and keeps the joint from stiffening.

⏱ Alphabet 1–2× + pumps 20 reps, 3–4 times a day

🏃 Stage 2 — band strengthening (4 directions)

Loop a band on the foot and lift up, push down, and push in and out. Pushing outward (peroneals) especially matters for preventing recurrence.

⏱ 15 reps each direction × 2 sets, once a day

🏃 Stage 3 — balance training (the core of rehab)

Single-leg stand 30 sec → eyes closed → on a pillow → catching a ball on one leg, raising difficulty. Retrains the damaged position sensors.

⏱ 5 minutes, daily (standing on one leg while brushing teeth works too)

🏃 Stage 4 — calf raises & return to jumping

Two-leg calf raise → single-leg → two-foot hop → single-leg hop → cutting hops, in order.

⏱ 10–15 reps × 2–3 sets each, every other day

🏃 Dorsiflexion mobility (knee-to-wall)

Foot a hand-width from a wall, keep the heel down, and touch the knee to the wall. When it touches, move the foot back further. Compare both sides until equal.

⏱ 10 reps × 3 sets, once a day

🚨 Stop self-care and see a doctor if…

  • Tenderness behind the ankle bone or inability to take four steps (suspect a fracture — X-ray)
  • Pain at the mid-inner foot (navicular) or the outer mid-foot bone (fracture-prone spots)
  • Numb or pale toes (nerve/vessel injury)
  • Still can't bear weight after 2–3 weeks
  • The ankle repeatedly buckles weakly (consider a ligament-reconstruction consult)

⚠️ This material is for health information only and does not replace a doctor's diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms last more than 2 weeks or any red flag applies, see a medical professional.
